What the Green Means in Manhattan

The question

What is the green across Manhattan actually made of, and does the pattern of high vegetation-index values at Landsat's 30 m scale track habitat, or only the density of maintained leaf surface?

The data

I took four index readings in Manhattan: Times Square, the Lake and Sheep Meadow in Central Park and the Battery Park. Three of them come from one Landsat 9 OLI scene, LC09_L1TP_014032_20260512 (path 14, row 32), acquired 12 May 2026, scene cloud cover 0%. The Battery reading comes from Landsat 8, LC08_L1TP_014032_20241021, acquired 21 October 2024, cloud cover 0%, because that was the scene the default mosaic served there.

Cell size is 30 m. The index is NDVI, computed as (b5 − b4) / (b5 + b4), where band 4 is red and band 5 is near-infrared, on apparent reflectance.

Source: Esri's Multispectral Landsat image service (ArcGIS Living Atlas), credited to Esri, USGS and NASA. It is public and token-free, so a reader without an ArcGIS account can open the same layer.

The method

In ArcGIS Map Viewer I switched the layer to the NDVI Raw processing template and clicked four places: Times Square, the Lake in and Sheep Meadow in Central Park, and the Battery Park. From the first page of each pop-up I recorded the pixel value, scene name, date and cloud cover. The map is displayed with NDVI Colorized.

In Landsat Explorer I used the spectral profile tool on one Central Park pixel on two dates. I also used the index mask with a lower threshold of 0.5, a round number of the kind a planning office would choose, so that the mask shows what a "0.5 counts as green" policy would certify.

What I found

Sheep Meadow in Central Park (approx. 40.772° N, 73.975° W) reads 0.840 on 12 May 2026, the highest value in my sample and close to the index's ceiling. It is a single open, mown and maintained lawn: uniform turf with no shrub layer and no canopy. It lights up under my 0.5 threshold as strongly as anywhere on the island, yet it is not habitat in any meaningful sense. The high score measures how dense and well kept the grass is.

Times Square reads 0.061 on the same image. Almost every 30 m cell there is pavement, roof or façade, with only scattered street trees, so the index finds almost no photosynthesising surface. That near-zero value says nothing about what lives there.

The Lake, a few hundred metres north of Sheep Meadow, reads −0.224. Water absorbs near-infrared, so the index goes negative. That is correct as measurement, but this open water is where the park's waterfowl feed and rest.

The Battery reads 0.312 on an October 2024 image. Its 30 m cells mix lawns, trees, planted beds and paved promenades, and the image is from autumn, so the middle value reflects both the mixture and the season.

Manhattan's green is mostly mown lawn and planted trees inside a few managed parks, and the distribution of high values follows maintenance, not habitat.

What this does not show

I am using the Landsat vegetation index across Manhattan to stand for how much living habitat is there, and it might fail because it gives Sheep Meadow's mown lawn (0.840) the highest score in my sample while scoring the Lake, open water used by waterfowl, as negative (−0.224).

This map is not evidence that Manhattan's greenest pixels are its most valuable habitat. It is also not one picture of one day.
