Two Routes for the Rain

The question

Where does the water in Lower Manhattan come from, where does the rain go after it hits the ground, and who else depends on the same water? A watershed map answers these through connectivity (what drains to where) and adjacency (which side of a divide I am on).

The data

Catchment: Global Watersheds (M. Heberger, mghydro.com), queried 13 October 2026. The main run used MERIT-Hydro (2021) at Lower precision. I compared it with MERIT-Hydro at Higher precision, HydroSHEDS (2013) and USGS (USA only). Unit: km² upstream of a snapped outlet.

Index: WRI Aqueduct 4.0 Water Risk, Baseline Annual and Baseline Monthly sublayers, via ArcGIS Living Atlas. CC BY 4.0, credited as “Aqueduct 4.0, World Resources Institute”. The baseline period is 1979–2019 and the data vintage is April 2023, so the figure describes a window that closed seven years ago. Unit: a HydroBASINS level-6 sub-basin, cut along state lines.

The method

Every run used the same input coordinate, 40.713, -74.004.

MERIT-Hydro snapped the outlet to 40.741, -74.008, about 3.1 km north on the Hudson near 14th Street. It returned 2 km² and reported “No river reaches were found.” My site lies outside this polygon. Higher precision returned the same 2 km².

HydroSHEDS returned 59 km². Its message gives my own coordinate as the outlet, but the map draws the snapped point near 40.79, -73.93 by the Harlem River, roughly 10 km away.

USGS returned no boundary.

Aqueduct places my site in North Atlantic Coast Basin, New York (Pfafstetter 731300). That polygon is 57.93 km², about 580 times my site if the site is taken as 0.1 km². The adjacent polygon, also in New York, is 176.12 km².

What I found

Wastewater from my block crosses the divide. According to MERIT-Hydro, rain on my block flows west to the Hudson. The sewer sends it east instead. Lower Manhattan’s combined sewers collect at the Thirteenth Street Pumping Station on Avenue D, and from there the water is pumped under the East River to the Newtown Creek treatment plant in Brooklyn (source: NYC DEP). The watershed map has no way of showing this, because it only knows the shape of the ground, not the pipes beneath it.

The three delineations did not agree. From the same point I got 2 km² (MERIT-Hydro), 59 km² (HydroSHEDS), and no result at all (USGS), a difference of about 30 times. The datasets were not arguing about where a ridge is. They chose different rivers. MERIT sent my water a few streets west to the Hudson. HydroSHEDS sent it about [10 km] north to the Harlem River, and drew a long, thin catchment from the Battery up into the Bronx, as if rain at the tip of Manhattan travels the length of the island before reaching water. Manhattan is so flat and built-over that small errors in elevation are enough to change the answer. In hilly cities like Prague, the datasets agree. Here, the choice of dataset decides the result.

The water stress index barely changes. My basin’s annual figure is 0.06 (Low). Month by month it ranges from 0.030 in April to 0.088 in October, about three times higher at its peak, but it never rises above 0.1, so the annual “Low” label is honest for every month. The neighbouring polygon reads 0.03, so the two belong to different sub-basins rather than being one basin split by a border.

What this does not show

I am using Aqueduct’s baseline water stress for my basin to stand for whether water at my site is in short supply. It might fail because the number describes the small coastal basin around Manhattan, but my tap water does not come from that basin. It arrives by tunnel from reservoirs more than 100 km upstate. A “Low” reading here says nothing about whether those distant reservoirs are under pressure.
