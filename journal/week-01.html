Population and Night Light Patterns Across Manhattan

The question

Where in New York City do higher population density and brighter nighttime lights occur together, and what does this suggest about the relationship between population and urban activity?

The data

The population density and the night lights layers are from Google Earth Engine Apps, using the 2026 layers. The population density layer shows where more people are concentrated across the city, while the night lights layer shows the intensity of artificial light at night.

I am using population density to stand for where people live, and it might fail because it does not capture people who are only present in an area temporarily. I am using nighttime light intensity to stand for human activity and development, and it might fail because lights can come from roads, commercial areas, or infrastructure even when few people live there.

The method

I used the NYC satellite imagery as the base layer and compared it with the population density and night light layers. I first looked at the population density layer to identify areas with higher concentrations of people, and then compare those areas with the brighter areas in the night light layer. I adjusted the layer opacity to about 50% so that the underlying map and the overlay could both be seen. I focused on the Manhattan area at a city-scale zoom to compare the spatial patterns of population density and nighttime lights within Manhattan.

What I found

There is a clear pattern that in which areas with higher population density tend to overlap with areas that have brighter night lights.
The distribution of population density is uneven: Manhattan has a high concentration of people compared with surrounding areas, with especially high population density around Central Park. In contrast, night lights are relatively bright across most of Manhattan rather than being concentrated in only some area.
The density of both population and nighttime lights is especially high in central and highly urbanized areas, while less densely populated areas generally have weaker nighttime light signals.

What this does not show

This comparison does not prove that high population density directly causes brighter nighttime lights. Night lights can also reflect roads, businesses, airports, infrastructure, and other forms of activity. Similarly, population density represents where people are estimated to live, not where they spend their time during the day.
For example, some areas on the western side of Manhattan near the waterfront and ports have relatively low population density but still appear quite bright in the night light layer. This suggests that night lights can capture human activity and infrastructure beyond residential population.
I also only focused on Manhattan, so the pattern might look different if I zoomed out and included the rest of New York City and surrounding areas. The results also depend on the accuracy and resolution of the two layers.
