The question

How does the terrain shape of my site in Manhattan influence the direction of surface water flow?

The data

Which layers you used in the Site Scanner, what years they are from, and who made them. Then the part people skip: what is missing from them, and what each layer is standing in for.

The method

I added the World Elevation Coverage Map first to identify the highest-resolution elevation dataset available at my site. I then added Terrain — Slope Degrees and Terrain — Slope Map, both derived from the same elevation service.

What I found

The slope maps show a clear spatial pattern in Manhattan’s terrain. Northern Manhattan contains more pronounced areas of higher slope, while much of Midtown and Lower Manhattan has relatively low slope values. In the continuous Slope Degrees map, most of Manhattan appears to have slopes below about 4°, with much of Lower Manhattan around 0–1° and parts of Midtown around 2–4°. Steeper areas, reaching roughly 6–12°, are especially visible around parts of Upper Manhattan near the Hudson River.

At a larger regional scale, the contrast becomes more apparent. Areas north and west of Manhattan contain much larger zones of steep terrain, corresponding to the more rugged topography around the Hudson Valley and surrounding uplands. The Hudson River runs through a major regional lowland, while areas farther north include the Hudson Highlands and other mountainous terrain.

This suggests that Manhattan is part of a larger regional pattern of ridges, valleys, and low-lying river corridors rather than being an isolated flat island. Within Manhattan, the northern high ground and lower-lying southern areas create a terrain gradient that could influence where surface runoff accumulates. However, the slope maps alone do not establish the exact direction of water flow. They show steepness, not aspect or drainage pathways.
