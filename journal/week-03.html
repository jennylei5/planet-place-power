The question

How does the terrain shape of my site in Manhattan influence the direction of surface water flow?

The data

I used Esri’s World Elevation Terrain service and the World Elevation Coverage Map to identify the elevation dataset covering Manhattan. The coverage map shows that Manhattan is covered by a 1 m elevation dataset, USGS_CoNED_TBDEM1m, produced by USGS. This dataset has a stated flight period of 1859–2020, while its LE90 is not stated. The 1 m cell size means that each elevation value represents a 1 m × 1 m area.

I also used SoilGrids to examine organic carbon density at 5-15 cm. Unlike the elevation layer, SoilGrids is a prediction map rather than a direct map of measurements, and it provides a prediction interval that indicates the uncertainty of the prediction. SoilGrids is published by ISRIC under CC BY 4.0.

The method

I added the World Elevation Coverage Map first to identify the highest-resolution elevation dataset available at my site. I then added Terrain — Slope Degrees and Terrain — Slope Map, both derived from the same elevation service.

Slope is not directly measured. Instead, the slope layer calculates the steepest gradient from a nine-cell neighborhood: one cell and its eight surrounding cells are used to fit a plane, and the resulting slope is assigned to the central cell. Because the calculation uses a nine-cell window, the resulting slope is dependent on the 1 m cell size.

The two slope layers use the same underlying values but represent them differently. Slope Degrees uses a continuous color ramp, while Slope Map classifies the values into discrete ranges. I also adjusted the color range and classification breaks in my own rendering so that the relatively small range of slopes in Manhattan was more visible. This changed the visual representation, not the underlying data.

For the soil analysis, I selected organic carbon densoty at depth 5-15 cm and recorded both the predicted value and its uncertainty.

What I found

The slope maps show a clear spatial pattern in Manhattan’s terrain. Northern Manhattan contains more pronounced areas of higher slope, while much of Midtown and Lower Manhattan has relatively low slope values. In the continuous Slope Degrees map, most of Manhattan appears to have slopes below about 4°, with much of Lower Manhattan around 0–1° and parts of Midtown around 2–4°. Steeper areas, reaching roughly 6–12°, are especially visible around parts of Upper Manhattan near the Hudson River.

At a larger regional scale, the contrast becomes more apparent. Areas north and west of Manhattan contain much larger zones of steep terrain, corresponding to the more rugged topography around the Hudson Valley and surrounding uplands. The Hudson River runs through a major regional lowland, while areas farther north include the Hudson Highlands and other mountainous terrain.

This suggests that Manhattan is part of a larger regional pattern of ridges, valleys, and low-lying river corridors rather than being an isolated flat island. Within Manhattan, the northern high ground and lower-lying southern areas create a terrain gradient that could influence where surface runoff accumulates. However, the slope maps alone do not establish the exact direction of water flow. They show steepness, not aspect or drainage pathways.

What this does not show

The map does not show actual drainage or flood pathways. Slope indicates the steepness of the terrain but not its aspect or exact flow direction. Urban drainage systems, including sewers, drains, walls, pumps, and tide gates, are not represented in the elevation grid. Therefore, this map cannot by itself predict where floodwater will accumulate during a storm.
